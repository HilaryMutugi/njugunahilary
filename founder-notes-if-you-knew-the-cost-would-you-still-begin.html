If You Knew the Cost, Would You Still Begin?

Njuguna HilaryFounder, AfrifamaNovember 13, 20265 min read

If you knew the cost—if you knew what it would take to get where you wanted, if you could see the disappointment and maybe the pain of getting there—would you still begin the journey?

That question found me a few nights ago in a film I was watching. It was meant to test a character’s resolve. It found me again this week as I went through an old notebook I’ve kept for almost seven years now.

I used to journal in it a lot. I still remember the first entry because there was a time when I couldn’t even afford a notebook, and someone gave me this one. Early on, I wrote a short letter to myself in it. I’ll get to that.

Before the letter, there is so much else in this book. Notes from startup books I was reading. Notes from a management meeting, back when I was still employed. Notes from my last day at that job, when I finally quit to chase entrepreneurship.

“I quit. I want to pursue my entrepreneurship dream. I want to start an ugly startup and be the change maker in the world. So help me God.”

There are pages I can barely read now. Words like equity, investing, hedging. Some of what I wrote there, I followed. Some of it I didn’t, and it came back to bite me.

I kept reading, kept listening, kept writing down whatever I found. There are notes from Strive Masiyiwa’s town hall meeting when he visited Strathmore in Nairobi. As the founder of Econet and Liquid, he has been hugely influential for me. There are notes on organisational models—egalitarian versus hierarchical structures, reward dilemmas, equity versus competition, static versus dynamic structures, founders’ natural inclinations—much of it drawn from Professor Noam Wasserman’s work. One day, I’m going to go back and read it all properly.

But the letter is what stopped me this week. I wrote it to myself almost ten years ago, back in 2016.

I’m telling you this because it isn’t every day you land on one of those milestones, the kind where life seems to double back on itself: eleven, twenty-two, thirty-three. I’ve just gone through a decade like that.

And where am I now? I’m still a founder. I’ve finally found my groove. I’ve started to travel more of the world and make the kind of impact I always looked forward to. But the drive is still there. The challenges are still there. The mental grind is still there too. Those are things I’m still learning to navigate.

“Dear Hilary Future. You’ve been doing impressive work with your agribusiness project, AfriFarm Model Farm. Two weeks ago, you planted your very first seed, and today you’re still planting. Your belief, aspiration and motivation are second to none. For that, I commend you. Don’t you read three books simultaneously? I used to read three books, unbelievable. You are a champion. You are an achiever. Keep believing and chasing your dreams. Don’t let naysayers clown your judgement or doubt your ability. Keep investing in knowledge, and one day you will achieve your dream. Sincerely, from Aaron Stealth, aspiring agripreneur, Hilary.”

Reading it back, I believe in affirmations more than I used to. A year ago, just after December, I was on edge—travelling back to my village empty-handed and frustrated, with only hope and God. There is nothing more painful than that.

But as an entrepreneur, I’ve learned that you’ll do whatever it takes to reach the mountaintop you’ve chosen. For me, that means being here, becoming. It’s not as easy as it looks from the outside. I’ve doubted myself more times than I can count. There was a time when I couldn’t afford much of anything. I declined job offers so I could keep pushing this forward. There are things about this road you can’t even say out loud because you never know who’s listening, so you simply say: help me, God.

So, back to the question from the film. If I’d known what this would take from me, would I still have chosen this path?

I don’t think I would have changed anything.

From the very beginning—back in school, when I was first introduced to entrepreneurship—I already saw myself as one. And I’m grateful for everyone who has walked part of this journey with me. Strangers from the other side of the world who have become part of the family; people who have seen the struggles and kept believing even without seeing results yet; people who have offered their insight and support along the way.

That is what keeps my faith in people alive. Anywhere, anyone—regardless of background—can get where they want to go if they are willing to chase their personal legend, as Paulo Coelho calls it, all the way to its deepest point.

That is something I used to get from reading Strive Masiyiwa, before he recently said he was taking a break because he felt he had given enough to young entrepreneurs in Africa. His writing gave me the feeling of sitting down with a mentor. It is part of why I keep writing on my own site and on social media: I want to do the same for someone else, not out of pride, but because you can share your story and help someone feel less alone in theirs.

It is also why I only write when I genuinely feel it, not simply for the sake of writing. If I’m not inspired, if my mind isn’t steady, I can’t write. And if I force it, you’ll be able to tell. I would rather have an honest conversation with you than a polished one.

There is a line from Wasserman that I keep coming back to: there is a lot of mystique around founders. People see them as having a “calling,” and while that is inspiring, it is not the whole picture. Different motivations lead to different decisions. Founders often don’t know exactly where they are going, don’t see the dangers ahead, and don’t recognise the forks in the road until they are already past them.

I think that is true. But I also don’t think you need to know the full cost before you start. If you knew everything upfront, it might talk you out of beginning.

What you need is to be motivated enough to chase it anyway, however lonely it gets.

If you truly give yourself to one thing, something in it finds a way to align in your favour.

This is Diary of an Entrepreneur. Until next time.
